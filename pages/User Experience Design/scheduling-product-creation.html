Scheduling Product Creation

How I turned an ambiguous mandate into a research-led scheduling product—and used weak beta adoption to redirect the strategy toward mobile.

RoleSole product designer
ScopeResearch through delivery
Initial designApproximately 2 months
PartnersProduct, engineering, and QA

In brief

I led the definition of a new scheduling product, then used weak beta adoption to identify a larger access problem and redirect the strategy toward mobile.

Challenge

Make variation manageable

Fire departments needed flexible rotations without the complexity of generic scheduling tools.

Direction

Build, learn, redirect

I defined the desktop MVP, tested the schedule engine, and reframed low adoption as a strategy signal.

Impact

From beta to paid product

The beta reached 100+ agencies; the mobile follow-up helped the product move beyond beta and generate revenue.

A market defined by exceptions

Paper and spreadsheets remained common because existing products were expensive, overbuilt, and unable to represent irregular rotations.

Paid departments

Stable teams

The same people often worked predictable rotating shifts.

Volunteer departments

Availability first

Leaders gathered availability, then built coverage around submissions.

Combination departments

Mixed staffing

Fixed paid crews were supplemented by volunteers as needed.

The product could not impose one “standard” schedule on a market defined by variation.

Defining a focused first release

The desktop MVP had to support real fire-service operations without becoming another dense configuration tool.

What the MVP had to enable

  • Create multiple schedules and rotating shifts
  • Start from common templates or customize a rotation
  • Create positions and personnel
  • Assign qualified people to positions on shifts

What we intentionally deferred

  • Self-service shift claiming and requests
  • Coverage notifications and mass communication
  • Detailed schedule history and printing
  • A native mobile experience

Four decisions that shaped the product

The work centered on reducing risk: understand the operating models, clarify the domain, test the schedule engine, and learn from adoption.

01 · Discover the real job

Research beyond feature requests

I interviewed six fire-service leaders and trialed four scheduling products to understand how coverage was assembled—not simply which features people requested.

Evidence that shaped the strategy

  • Pen, paper, and spreadsheets remained common
  • Irregular rotations were the core unmet need
  • Departments shared concepts, but not one workflow
  • Too many configuration options made competitors difficult to use
02 · Model the domain

Turn operational complexity into a clear system

I defined how the core objects related, giving product and engineering a shared language before detailed interface work began.

Core product model

Schedule → Shift → Position → Person
03 · Test the riskiest assumption

Use failure to improve the schedule engine

Testing with four fire-service leaders validated the language and flow—but exposed that the schedule engine could not represent enough real rotations.

Decision after testing

  • Make the rotation itself directly configurable
  • Preview the pattern before saving
  • Ship 24-hour shifts first to protect the timeline
  • Keep variable shift lengths in the near-term roadmap
04 · Respond to adoption

Reframe a weak beta as a strategy signal

Low beta adoption revealed a problem larger than polish: non-admin users could not participate easily, and customers expected mobile access.

How the strategy changed

Desktop beta Low adoption More interviews Mobile access gap 10-day mobile MVP

What changed after launch

The first release did not validate every assumption. It did expose the access model the product needed to serve people beyond department administrators.

100+

Agencies in desktop beta

The initial product reached a meaningful customer cohort within three months.

10 days

To design the mobile MVP

A focused native experience addressed onboarding, account access, and schedule visibility.

2021

Moved beyond beta

The scheduling platform became a revenue-generating product for Active911.

Senior retrospective

What I would do differently today

  • Include administrators, volunteers, and paid personnel in discovery from the start—not only department leadership
  • Define the adoption funnel and product-success measures before beta
  • Validate the access model and cross-platform experience before investing deeply in desktop workflows
  • Frame the MVP around a complete critical job, not only a prioritized feature list
