User Experience Design · U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs

PDF Digitization

Helping disabled Veterans’ families apply for health care through a clearer, accessible digital experience.

Overview

A legacy print-and-mail application created friction for beneficiaries and required extensive manual processing by VA case workers. Our team transformed it into an accessible, end-to-end digital workflow on VA.gov.

Context

The application was a print-only PDF. Applicants filled it out by hand and mailed it to the VA, where case workers manually interpreted and processed it.

Objective

Lead the transformation of a paper-based PDF process into an accessible digital workflow on VA.gov.

Challenge

Replace a legacy workflow with an intuitive experience while reducing the processing burden on VA staff.

My role

  • Main design point of contact for the project
  • Led research, interaction design, prototyping, and high-fidelity design
  • Contributed patterns to the VA design system
  • Helped manage stakeholders and cross-functional collaboration
  • Created developer annotations with accessibility guidance

Challenges and opportunities

The paper process was hard for applicants to navigate and equally difficult for VA staff to process. Digitization could remove barriers on both sides of the service.

Challenges
Opportunities
Customer
  • Mailing printed PDFs was cumbersome
  • Instructions and application help were difficult to access
  • Thousands of people apply every year despite these barriers
  • A guided digital flow could make the process more approachable
Business
  • Manual sorting led to mistakes
  • Handwriting caused missing signatures, ineligible entries, and processing delays

Approach

We started lean, addressed issues as they emerged, tested with users, analyzed findings, and incorporated improvements in a mobile-first experience.

High-level strategy

Start small, learn continuously, and improve the experience as evidence emerged.

Methodologies

Human-centered design, inclusive and accessible design principles, and agile teamwork.

Collaboration

Regular check-ins with design, engineering, and stakeholders, with a shared design decision log.

Process

I moved from service-level understanding to detailed interaction design, testing each stage and preparing the final experience for an accessible engineering handoff.

01 · Map the service

Updated user flow

I cleaned up the existing user flow to make it flatter, clearer, and more streamlined. I then facilitated a Figma workshop so the team could collaboratively build the first section using the VA design system.

02 · Learn and structure

Stakeholder interviews and wireframes

I interviewed researchers and stakeholders to uncover the right questions and update the user flow. I translated the flow into wireframes using the existing VA design library.

03 · Increase fidelity

Screen advancement and clickable prototype

I brought the core questions and concerns into each screen, then connected the high-fidelity designs into a clickable prototype for stakeholder review and usability testing.

04 · Test and hand off

User testing, iteration, and annotations

I supported post-interview analysis and report writing, then updated the flow and screens based on feedback. For handoff, I documented the golden path, auxiliary screens, responsive states, and accessibility requirements.

Proposed solution

A guided, mobile-first application on VA.gov that made eligibility, applicant information, document collection, review, and submission easier to understand.

Design decisions

User research and testing shaped a set of interaction patterns that reduced cognitive load, supported complex family situations, and made document submission more flexible.

Mobile-first

Design for the smallest screen first

Most VA applicants use mobile devices. Starting with the smallest screens made it easier to prioritize content, then adapt the experience to larger screens.

Progressive disclosure

Show only what is relevant

Questions were ordered so applicants only saw follow-up questions when they applied. For example, document-upload guidance appeared only when a scenario required supporting evidence.

Dynamic content

Use each applicant’s perspective

I moved signer information to the beginning so later content could use the applicant’s role and name—for example, changing “Veteran information” to “Your information” when appropriate.

Document flexibility

Allow optional documents

Stakeholder interviews showed that applicants could provide many possible document types, but very few were universally required. The upload flow kept these options available without turning them into barriers.

Upload timing

Ask for files when they become relevant

The file prompt appears at the moment a document becomes relevant, with the option to skip it and return during review.

Address pattern

Reduce repeated address entry

Testing showed that people were frustrated when entering the same address for multiple people in one household. A reusable-address pattern removed that friction while still supporting different addresses.

List and loop

Make repeated applicants manageable

Instead of one inefficient repeating pattern, applicants first create a list of people and then complete each person’s questions individually. A submitted version of this pattern later replaced the older design in the VA design system.

Review

Give documents a clear overview

A dedicated review page gives applicants another opportunity to upload files before submission. Existing VA design-system components separate content by applicant and document requirements.

Mail-in alternative

Keep a non-digital path available

Some applicants told us they would rather mail documents than upload them. The experience keeps that option visible while allowing processors to continue working with the rest of the application.

Post-submission

Make application status unmistakable

After submission, the interface clearly distinguishes fully submitted applications from those still missing required documentation. Existing alert patterns help applicants see what happened and what to do next.

Outcomes

The service launched in fall 2024 after two previously failed attempts and immediately demonstrated demand for a digital application.

Results and metrics

  • Launched in fall 2024
  • 24,000 applications submitted online in the first two months

What I learned

  • Aligning on processes before designing saves time later
  • There is no substitute for in-depth user research
  • Balancing stakeholder requests with prioritized design needs is an art form

User impact

  • More people can apply through a more accessible method
  • The guided flow is easier to complete than the PDF
  • Applicants can apply without leaving home

Business impact

  • More applications can be processed in less time
  • Staff no longer need to decipher handwriting or rely on physical mail
  • Uploaded files arrive directly and are easier to review

A clearer digital service allows more people to apply—and helps processors approve applications faster, with fewer mistakes.
