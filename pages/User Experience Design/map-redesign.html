User Experience Design · Campendium

Map Overlay Redesign

Helping RVers efficiently explore, compare, and navigate map overlays.

Overview

Campendium’s main map split overlay controls across three buttons and four different drawers. When the team needed to add a smoke layer, I used the opportunity to redesign the system rather than add another disconnected control.

Context

Map layers had grown into a fragmented collection of controls, making the interface harder to understand and extend.

Objective

Improve the user experience, increase Pro upsell conversion, and establish a system that could support future layers.

Challenge

Create a smooth, intuitive way to select, compare, and change multiple map layers.

My role

I led the design work and was responsible for the project from early concept development through developer handoff.

Challenges and opportunities

The redesign needed to reduce customer confusion while creating a clearer, more scalable place for Campendium’s free and Pro map tools.

Approach

I worked mobile-first, explored multiple UI patterns, and adapted the strongest direction for desktop and tablet.

High-level strategy

Design for mobile first, adapt for larger devices, and experiment until the interaction felt efficient and natural.

Methodologies

Human-centered design principles supported by a lean product framework.

Collaboration

Regular check-ins with product and engineering, plus recurring internal design reviews.

Process

I combined competitive research, customer knowledge, information architecture, rapid prototyping, and visual exploration to arrive at a flexible interaction system.

01 · Research the landscape

State-of-the-art research

I reviewed dozens of mapping and navigation apps to understand familiar interaction patterns, useful conventions, and opportunities to improve on the status quo.

02 · Organize the system

Inventory and sorting

I inventoried current and planned overlays, then grouped them into categories that could remain understandable as the product grew.

03 · Understand RVers

Interviews and user journey

I spoke with full-time RVers about how they use maps and what information they need. I combined those conversations with existing product knowledge to map a detailed journey and identify moments where users compare multiple map types.

04 · Explore the interface

Wireframes and visual experimentation

I borrowed effective patterns from other products and explored several visual directions, balancing aesthetic quality with fast, efficient selection.

05 · Prototype and extend

Interaction design and additional states

I used Figma’s prototyping tools to test which interactions communicated the design best. Once the core system was resolved, I adapted it for desktop, Android, dark mode, and locked states.

Proposed solution

A single map drawer brings base maps and every overlay into one organized, scalable interface across mobile and desktop.

Design decisions

The final system combines familiar drawer behavior with clearer grouping, flexible selection patterns, and stronger feedback about the user’s current map state.

Mobile-first

Start where most people use the feature

Most customers use the app on mobile. Starting with the smallest screen clarified priorities and made the interaction easier to expand for desktop than to compress later.

Consolidation

Replace three buttons with one entry point

A single map control looks cleaner and gives every overlay a consistent home inside one drawer.

Drawer pattern

Keep behavior users already understand

The existing drawer supported internal scrolling, different screen heights, and thumb-friendly use. Keeping it preserved expected behavior and made the redesign feel familiar.

Tile pattern

Make options visual and easy to tap

Image-and-text tiles provide a large target, preview each overlay, and feel familiar. I added shadows for contrast, strengthened the image-label relationship, and narrowed the tiles so three fit across.

Information architecture

Use categories to reduce cognitive load

Base maps, map overlays, public lands, and environmental information are grouped into internal categories with familiar names, making a growing collection easier to scan.

Base maps

Make single selection unmistakable

A blue border, rounded shadow, and reduced saturation on unselected tiles clarify which base map is active. The full tile is clickable because only one base map can be selected at a time.

Overlays

Support multiple simultaneous selections

Overlay tiles look related to base maps but use checkboxes to communicate that several can be active at once. Full-color unselected tiles remain visibly available for interaction.

Selection feedback

Keep the current state visible

A count pill shows how many overlays are selected, even when an active tile has been scrolled out of view.

Bulk action

Reveal “Clear all” only when it is useful

The action appears after at least one selection is made, giving users a fast reset without cluttering the default state.

Outcomes and impact

The redesign launched to immediate positive feedback, created a clearer way to compare overlays, and gave the business a stronger home for Pro features.

Results and metrics

The experience launched successfully, and users immediately told the team they loved it.

Business impact

The product manager reported increased conversion and engagement, though exact metrics were not available.

User impact

A consolidated view makes overlays easier to compare and combine, while presenting all Pro options in one understandable place.

What I learned

  • Collaboration and adaptability matter when joining a team as the newcomer

A scalable foundation

The new structure gives Campendium a consistent system for adding future base maps, overlays, and paid features without returning to fragmented controls.

One organized map system replaced a collection of disconnected controls—making exploration easier for RVers and future growth easier for the product team.
