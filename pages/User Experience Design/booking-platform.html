Product Design · Campendium · 2022

Campground Booking Platform

Designing a trusted, end-to-end marketplace experience that brought campground discovery, site selection, payment, and confirmation into one product.

Overview

Campendium had a large audience discovering campgrounds, but reservations happened elsewhere. The company saw an opportunity to create a new revenue stream by connecting discovery directly to booking. I led product design for the marketplace experience, from early framing through launch.

Opportunity

Turn high-intent campground discovery into a direct reservation without sending travelers to a third party.

Design challenge

Add a transactional product to a content-focused platform without disrupting the behaviors customers already understood.

Business constraint

The experience needed to work with a new and initially limited supply of bookable properties.

My role and scope

As the product designer, I owned the experience strategy, research, service flow, interaction model, responsive UI, prototyping, and annotated handoff. I partnered closely with product management and engineering to sequence the work, resolve technical constraints, and define a shippable first version in roughly two months.

Strategic framing

This was not simply a checkout-page project. It introduced a new marketplace model and required Campendium to support customers from initial discovery through post-purchase reassurance. I framed the work as one connected service rather than a collection of screens.

Experience hypothesis

If bookable inventory felt native to Campendium and customers could verify that a site fit their trip, they would be more willing to complete a reservation directly.

Definition of success

A customer could identify an eligible property, find a compatible site, understand the full cost, complete payment, and retain a trustworthy record of the reservation.

Key product risk

A polished funnel would not create marketplace value without enough campground supply. The design therefore needed to scale beyond the launch inventory.

Experience principles

Four principles helped the team make consistent decisions across search, site qualification, checkout, and confirmation.

01

Signal availability early

Customers should understand which properties can be booked before investing time in evaluation.

02

Build confidence progressively

Reveal the right site, equipment, policy, and pricing details as commitment increases.

03

Feel native to Campendium

Extend familiar patterns instead of introducing a disconnected booking product.

04

Design the whole service

Treat discovery, transaction, confirmation, edge cases, and handoff as one system.

Research and sensemaking

I used the existing experience, competitive products, and workflow modeling to understand where Campendium could follow convention—and where its context required a different approach.

Existing service

The handoff to third parties broke trust and continuity

The existing path moved customers between products with different interaction models and unclear ownership. The opportunity was not just fewer clicks; it was preserving context and confidence across the entire journey.

Competitive research

Customers did not expect one universal booking flow

Research across Recreation.gov, Airbnb, KOA, and other overnight-stay products showed that booking models varied widely. That gave us permission to build around Campendium’s strongest context: customers often begin with a specific campground and need to qualify an individual site.

Synthesis

Purchase confidence depended on compatibility, not just availability

A campsite is not interchangeable inventory. Customers needed to know whether their equipment would fit, what hookups and access were available, which rules applied, and what the full stay would cost. I used those confidence requirements to define the information architecture.

Experience architecture

I translated the research into a four-stage funnel. Each stage answered a different customer question and reduced uncertainty before asking for greater commitment.

Discover

Can I book this?

Identify bookable properties and provide a clear path into availability.

Qualify

Will this site work?

Compare dates, capacity, equipment fit, amenities, access, and price.

Transact

What am I agreeing to?

Review guest details, policies, fees, and payment in one coherent view.

Reassure

What happens next?

Confirm success and provide a durable, accessible reservation record.

Key design decisions

The final experience emerged from a set of system-level decisions about visibility, information hierarchy, commitment, and technical resilience.

Decision 01 · Discovery

Make bookable inventory visible without fragmenting search

I added a consistent bookable signal and “Check Availability” action to existing search-result patterns, then prioritized eligible properties in the result order. This introduced the new business model while preserving the product’s familiar map-and-list behavior.

Decision 02 · Qualification

Turn campsite cards into decision-support tools

Price and availability alone were not enough. I extended Campendium’s card system to surface the details that determine real-world fit—capacity, rig dimensions, hookups, access, surface type, amenities, and policies—without forcing customers to open every option.

Preserve consistency while adding a new information layer

Reusing the existing card foundation reduced learning cost and implementation complexity. A clear visual hierarchy allowed customers to scan critical constraints first and move into richer detail only when needed.

Decision 03 · Search interaction

Keep date and guest selection in the campground context

Rather than create another destination page, I placed availability controls where customers were already evaluating the campground. This kept property context visible and made the transition from browsing to booking feel like a natural progression.

Decision 04 · Checkout

Choose reviewability over artificial step reduction

I initially explored a three-step checkout to reduce cognitive load. Journey analysis showed that customers had already completed several decisions before payment, and separating the remaining information made review harder. I moved to one page so customers could understand and edit the complete reservation in context.

A deliberate tradeoff

The page was longer, but it reduced navigation, made fees and policies continuously visible, and supported comparison between entered details and the selected stay. The design prioritized confidence over the appearance of simplicity.

Decision 05 · Confirmation

Design reassurance as part of the product—not an afterthought

The confirmation experience mirrored the checkout hierarchy so customers could quickly verify the reservation. When email-platform constraints made the original concept infeasible, I worked directly with engineering to simplify the structure and preserve clarity across clients, screen sizes, and light and dark modes.

Leadership and delivery

The scope crossed discovery, commerce, legal requirements, responsive web, payment, and email. Keeping the work moving required shared models and intentional cross-functional decision-making.

Sequenced the system

I partnered with product management to break the service into independently buildable phases without losing the integrity of the end-to-end journey.

Designed with engineering

Early technical conversations shaped the date selector, checkout architecture, email markup, responsive behavior, and error handling.

Made decisions traceable

The handoff documented the golden path, behavior, component details, breakpoints, validation, and unsuccessful-purchase states.

Outcomes

The platform launched in May 2022 as a complete booking service. The design solved the customer journey, but the marketplace launched with only seven registered properties and the company could not recruit supply quickly enough to sustain the model.

Experience outcome

A coherent path connected campground discovery to site selection, checkout, and confirmation within Campendium.

Operational reality

Property-management constraints limited launch inventory to seven properties across the United States.

Business outcome

After resource constraints and organizational layoffs, the marketplace was ultimately sunset.

Reflection

The project strengthened how I think about product design beyond the interface: a strong customer experience is necessary, but it cannot compensate for an unsupported marketplace or operating model.

Design the service and the conditions around it

Supply, operations, customer support, legal requirements, and technical systems all shape whether an experience can succeed.

Use artifacts to create alignment

Journey maps, principles, inventories, and decision logs were most valuable as shared tools for making tradeoffs—not as deliverables by themselves.

Make complexity legible

Senior design leadership is often the work of turning a broad, ambiguous system into decisions a team can understand, sequence, build, and evaluate together.
